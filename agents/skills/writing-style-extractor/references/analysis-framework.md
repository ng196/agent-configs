# Style Analysis Framework

Detailed rubric for Step 3 of the writing-style-extractor skill. Work through each dimension; skip any that genuinely don't apply to the source (e.g. no dialogue in a non-fiction essay).

## 1. Sentence architecture
- Typical sentence length, and how much it varies within a paragraph
- Simple vs. compound vs. complex sentence ratio
- Use of sentence fragments for effect
- Where clauses tend to pile up (front-loaded vs. back-loaded)

*Weak entry:* "Uses varied sentence lengths."
*Strong entry:* "Alternates short declarative sentences (4–8 words) with one long, comma-spliced sentence per paragraph that trails off mid-thought — the short ones land like punches, the long one is where the interiority happens."

## 2. Vocabulary & diction
- Register: formal / casual / mixed
- Concrete vs. abstract word preference
- Any recurring words, verbs, or phrasings that show up often enough to be a signature
- Where the vocabulary gets unusually specific (technical terms, sensory detail, slang)

## 3. POV, tense, and narrative distance
- First / third / second person, past / present tense
- Close third vs. distant third if applicable
- How much interiority (direct access to a character's thoughts) is shown vs. implied through action

## 4. Dialogue conventions
- Tag style: plain "said," expressive tags ("she hissed"), or mostly untagged
- Use of action beats instead of tags
- How interruptions, overlapping speech, or silence are rendered
- Punctuation quirks around dialogue (em dashes for interruption, ellipses for trailing off)

## 5. Pacing & structure
- How scenes/chapters typically open (in medias res, scene-setting, a line of dialogue)
- How they typically close (cliffhanger, quiet beat, summary)
- Paragraph length patterns — dense blocks vs. frequent breaks
- Use of white space, section breaks, or formatting for pacing

## 6. Imagery & figurative language
- Frequency and type of metaphor/simile
- Recurring image clusters (nature, machinery, weather, the body, etc.)
- Whether imagery is decorative or load-bearing (does it carry meaning, or just texture)

## 7. Tone & emotional register
- Default emotional temperature (wry, earnest, melancholic, deadpan, etc.)
- How humor is deployed, if at all
- How the writing handles high-stakes/emotional moments — restraint vs. full expression

## 8. Punctuation & formatting quirks
- Em dash / semicolon / ellipsis habits
- Paragraph length as a formatting choice
- Any idiosyncratic habits (single-sentence paragraphs for emphasis, deliberate sentence fragments, unconventional capitalization)

---

## Example: turning "bad examples" into an Avoid section

If the user says "don't write like this" and pastes a flat, exposition-heavy passage, don't just say "avoid flat writing." Extract the *specific* mechanism:

*Weak:* "Avoid boring, generic prose."
*Strong:* "Avoid stacking three or more scene-setting sentences before any character speaks or acts — the sample material opens paragraphs by describing the room, then the weather, then the character's clothing, before anything happens. Cut straight to action or dialogue instead."

## Example: a filled-out Quick Checklist

- Open scenes on action or dialogue, not description
- Keep interiority close-third, not distant
- One long spiraling sentence per emotional beat, surrounded by short ones
- Dialogue tags: mostly "said," action beats over adverbs
- No more than two sentences of pure description before returning to a character
- Avoid resolving tension too quickly — let a beat sit for a paragraph
